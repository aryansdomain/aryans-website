Poly × Trig = Linear?

Feb 18, 2025

Intro

I was watching a youtube video the other day, and the guy was talking about this particular limit:

\[ \lim_{x \to 0} \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \cdot x^2\]

The solution was simple - use the squeeze theorem, x squared, yada yada. I graphed up the function to see the erratic behavior of it as x approaches 0, and I found something really fun:

If you haven't figured it out by now, the zoomed out graph looks almost exactly like a line, specifically f(x) = x. I found nothing about this online so I thought it would be fun to post about it here!

So... how does this happen?

We're trying to show that

\[ \sin(\frac{1}{x}) \cdot x^2\ = x, \text{ for large } x\]

1. Good Ol' Plugging and Chugging

First I tried plugging infinity into the equation (the largest x possible), but that just resulted in \( 0 \cdot \infty \). Very disappointing :(

2. Shake It Off... to the 3rd Term

Let's use our good old friend Taylor to expand \( \sin(y) \) around \( 0 \):

\[ \sin(y) \approx y - \frac{y^3}{6} + O(\frac{y^5}{24}). \]

Setting \( y = \frac{1}{x} \), we get:

\[ \sin(\frac{1}{x}) \approx \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{6x^3} + O(\frac{1}{24x^5}). \]

Now, multiplying both sides by \( x^2 \), we get:

\[ \sin(\frac{1}{x}) \cdot x^2 \approx x - \frac{1}{6x} - O(\frac{1}{24x^3}). \]

Now, if we plug in a huge number for x, we see all those terms (even the remainder terms, since they just have higher powers of x in their denominators) beautifully vanish, leaving

\[ \sin(\frac{1}{x}) \cdot x^2\ = x, \text{ for large } x.\]

3. The BORING solution that NOBODY likes

During the coding of this post, I noticed that many of you NERDS will be screaming at be just to divide \( x^2 \) from both sides, giving

\[ \sin(\frac{1}{x}) \approx \frac{1}{x}\]

And you can obviously see why this works if you plug in a large x.

You guys might be saying "This is obvious, just start with \(\sin(x) \approx x\) and do some algebra, but isn't that the case with all identities and equations like \(e^{i \pi} + 1 = 0\)? All I found here was a way to backwards-transform a cool fact into something even cooler - a complex equation looks extremely simple when you zoom out the graph :)

Something Extra

Now, as a little exercise to the reader, try seeing what happens when you replace the sine with cosine.

For a cooler method, try taking the derivative of both sides of our last equation.
